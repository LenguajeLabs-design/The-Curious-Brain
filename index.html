First, the most important part

You are still you.

ADHD is a name for a brain that may need extra help with things like steering attention, remembering steps, waiting, or sitting still. It does not mean you are lazy, bad, or broken. You did not cause it.

ADHD can look different

There isn’t one “ADHD kind of kid.”

Some people find focus tricky. Some feel extra wiggly or act before they mean to. Some experience a mix. Your brain has its own pattern, and the grown-ups helping you can learn what works for you.

Wondering about something?

Questions kids ask

Open any question that feels useful. It’s okay if you have more questions later.

Did I do something to get ADHD?

No. ADHD is not caused by being naughty, not trying, or making mistakes. You deserve help and kindness, just like any kid.

Does ADHD mean I’m not smart?

No. ADHD doesn’t tell anyone how smart, funny, caring, creative, or good at learning you are. It can make some tasks take more effort, and the right support can help.

Why did I find out now?

Sometimes school, friendships, or growing-up jobs ask for more planning and focus. That can make a brain’s support needs easier to notice. Now your team has a name for what’s going on and can make a plan with you.

Will I still be me if I take medicine?

Yes. Medicine is one possible tool to make some moments easier. You are still you—with your own jokes, favorite things, opinions, and big ideas. Tell your grown-up how it feels for you.

Do I have to tell my friends?

You can choose what you share and with whom. You could simply say, “I do better when I get one direction at a time,” without explaining anything else. A trusted grown-up can help you decide.

What if I feel worried, mad, or sad about this?

Those feelings are allowed. You don’t have to figure them out alone. Tell a grown-up you trust: “I have a question about ADHD and I want you to listen.”

Keep this close

The word “disorder” can sound big.

In a doctor’s name, it describes a pattern that can make some parts of life harder. It is not a description of your whole self.

“I can learn what helps my brain.”

You don’t have to feel brave every minute. Asking questions is a brave enough start.

Small experiments

A few tools to try

Pick one idea with a grown-up. Keep it if it helps; change it if it doesn’t.

1

Make the first step tiny

Instead of “do all the homework,” try “open the notebook and do the first question.” Starting is a real step.

2

Put the reminder where you’ll see it

Try a short checklist, a picture, or a note by your backpack. Let the reminder do some of the remembering.

3

Move, then return

A stretch, a quick walk, or carrying something to another room can be a planned movement break.

Want a few more ideas? Show 3 more.
4

Ask for one direction

Try: “Can you tell me the first thing to do?” You can ask for the next step when you’re ready.

5

Work near a helper

Sometimes doing your own task beside a calm grown-up makes it easier to begin and keep going.

6

Make a “launch pad”

Choose one spot for tomorrow’s backpack, shoes, and important things. A home for your stuff means less searching.

Remember: Tools are experiments, not tests. If one doesn’t fit you, that’s useful information.

A medicine question

What is my medicine for?

Your prescriber and pharmacist are your medicine experts. You can ask them anything.

A grown-up can check the exact name on your bottle

It may help some brain jobs feel easier.

If your prescription is a generic for Concerta, the bottle may say methylphenidate extended-release. Long-acting versions release medicine over time. Ask a grown-up to check the exact name and directions on your bottle with the pharmacist.

  • It might help with paying attention, pausing, or getting started.
  • People can feel different from one another, so tell your grown-up what you notice.
  • Some kids notice a tummy ache, a headache, less hunger, or trouble sleeping. Tell your grown-up so they can talk with your prescriber.
  • Take it only the way your grown-up and the label say. Don’t change it or share it.

Questions you can ask

You’re part of the team.

“What should I notice if it’s helping?”

“What should I tell you if something feels weird?”

“Can we check the bottle together?”

If something feels really wrong or you have trouble breathing, tell an adult right away.

A few people you might know

ADHD belongs in all kinds of stories

These people have talked publicly about ADHD. Their stories are theirs; your story gets to be yours.

Gymnastics · Olympic champion

Simone Biles

Simone has spoken about having ADHD and how focus could be tricky during gymnastics competitions. She kept training, found ways to manage it, and won many Olympic and world championship medals.

A grown-up can find an interview in the sources below.

Weightlifting · Olympic medalist

Kate Nye

Kate has talked about having ADHD. She won an Olympic silver medal in weightlifting. Her life is much bigger than either of those facts.

A grown-up can find a profile in the sources below.

Being famous or winning medals is not the goal for everyone. People with ADHD make, learn, help, play, and grow in millions of different ways.
